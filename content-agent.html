Content-Agent: Building an AI System That Researches, Fact-Checks, and Publishes Technical Articles

An engineering case study in grounding, evaluation, and human-in-the-loop publishing.

Jump to the grounding investigation ↓

Python LangGraph DeepSeek Qdrant + BM25 + RRF FastAPI SQLite Docker Caddy LangSmith

I built Content Agent to research and draft technical articles. It identifies the claims in a draft, checks them against the available evidence, and either revises eligible drafts or stops the run when the checks cannot establish support.

TL;DR

Content Agent is a LangGraph workflow that retrieves sources, drafts, checks claims, revises within limits, and asks a person to review both content and layout. Git operations are guarded, and a person initiates the final push. The video shows an earlier supervised demo; the project continues to evolve.

The Problem

A fluent technical article can make a precise claim sound convincing even when its sources do not support it. I wanted a way to find those claims, check the evidence behind them, and keep the useful substance instead of improving a score by making the writing vague.

What Content Agent Does

Given a topic, the agent gathers web and knowledge-base sources, drafts an article, and checks its claims against the evidence. It can revise within a bounded quality episode, then pauses for content review and later for layout review. Checkpoints preserve state across those pauses, and guarded Git operations prepare an approved article for a person to publish.

Investigation 1: Grounding Quality

Problem

Fluent drafts made precise claims that the retrieved sources did not support. A low unverified rate alone could be achieved by making the article vague.

Decision

Retrieve before drafting, ask the writer to use the available sources, and supply specific claim-level feedback for eligible revisions. Track substantive verified claims (SV) alongside unverified rate (UVR).

Evidence

In a historical multi-agent topic experiment, UVR fell from 0.63 to 0.22 and SV rose 253%. The pre-registered criterion failed, and I documented the decision to proceed as a judgment call. The result describes that experiment, not general reliability. M3 decision.

SV asks how much useful, supported substance remains; UVR measures the fraction judged unverified. Neither substitutes for complete claim coverage or material and citation policy. A later live run showed why: a score could look good while important claims still failed.

Investigation 2: Retrieval and Evidence Exposure

I moved from ChromaDB to Qdrant after concurrency problems in earlier benchmark work. Later, I found a subtler issue: chunks sized with one tokenizer could be truncated by the embedding model's tokenizer. A relevant document can rank highly while the exact sentence needed for verification is missing from the model's context.

The Phase 5 path uses dense MiniLM and lexical BM25, combined with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF). Contiguous Structural Window Packing (CSWP) respects document structure. Same-document neighbors expand the retrieved seeds, and a bounded, seed-first pack passes evidence to the drafter and verifier. Local and Qdrant serving are separately parity-tested; the Phase 5B3 experiment used the local path.

Investigation 3: Verification Integrity

Checking a claim is more than asking a model whether a sentence sounds right. Call A inventories claims and materiality from the draft. Call B assesses the complete roster against the evidence, including support and contradiction. Python then validates the identities, evidence bindings, material requirements and citations.

A small illustrative example
1
Draft claim“The method reduced latency by 20%.”
2
Call AInventory the statement, bind it to the draft and mark whether it is material.
3
Call BAssess whether the cited source supports, weakens or contradicts it, with an exact evidence quote.
4
Python checksCheck the full roster, current draft identity, quote binding, material policy and citation placement. Missing or mismatched evidence cannot be treated as a pass.

If the draft changes, an old verification result is stale: its claims may no longer match the text. Quote binding checks that the claimed supporting words actually appear in the stated source. Complete-roster enforcement prevents an omitted claim from disappearing from the assessment. These checks constrain model output; they do not prove a model's interpretation is always correct.

A citation defect and repair: A November 3 live run stopped when two distinct material claims had nested, overlapping draft anchors. I reproduced the failure from its saved checkpoint and corrected citation placement to preserve both source bindings at distinct endpoints. Offline replay passed the placement check, but seven material claims remained unresolved; the repair was not an end-to-end success. Failure and repair evidence

Investigation 4: Reliability Under Provider Execution

Provider responses can be malformed, truncated or lack usable usage data. The integrated workflow uses bounded attempts, checks response integrity, preserves checkpoints, and records unknown usage rather than inventing a cost. For a specific completed quote-binding miss, a separately bounded Call B recovery uses a durable journal so a restart does not silently duplicate a request.

Failed live run, November 5: Run 34cc2a46 reached Call B on its second draft: 39 claims verified and 18 weak. Its grounding score was 0.921 and UVR was 0.0, yet 10 material claims remained unresolved. The run ended at the quality limit before reflection, either human gate or publication. Good aggregate numbers did not authorize an unsafe article. Run record

Another run stopped when a claim's draft anchor matched two places. Guessing which occurrence was intended would compromise evidence identity, so the integrated guard fails closed. Separately, draft PR #11 proposes a larger bounded Call B output allowance and a stop on unusable verifier output after a truncated response. It is not part of merged Phase 5. These checks do not qualify a complete provider-backed article.

Investigation 5: Testing, Review, and Publishing

ChecksCI and failure injection

Lint, the test suite and a deterministic semantic evaluation gate check code and contracts. Failure-injection tests exercise issues such as timeouts and malformed responses. These do not replace a live provider-backed run.

Reviewcontent, then layout

Authenticated access and two genuine human decisions protect the route to publication. Layout revision loops back to layout review with content protected; rejection ends the run.

Publishguarded Git and rollback

GitPython writes to a branch and performs guarded local Git operations. A person initiates the final push, including via the authenticated publish endpoint; Netlify serves the resulting static site. The workflow includes tags and a revert-based rollback path.

Observability and Monitoring

Logs, checkpoints and telemetry help reconstruct a run, but crash telemetry can lack mid-run evidence and does not establish the exact code identity on its own.

  • Structured logs. Every node logs JSON to stdout through a shared logger, tagged with the run ID. In a container, stdout is the log sink.
  • Per-run telemetry. JSON records can include grounding reports, retrieved sources, per-iteration metrics, attribution, usage and latency. Available evidence depends on how far the run progressed.
  • Prompt-hash versioning. Runtime prompt hashes are recorded to help identify changes that affect comparisons between runs. Matching a verifier prompt alone does not establish that every other experimental condition matched.
  • Claim-level attribution. Source attribution records whether citations resolve to retrieved evidence. An unresolved attribution is a signal to investigate, not proof by itself of why a citation failed.
  • LangSmith tracing. When configured and enabled, tracing adds cross-node visibility and model-call usage with application-estimated cost when available. Estimates are not provider billing.

Automated alerting and dashboards were outside the original single-operator demo scope. Its runbook described a manually configured HTTP health monitor; the former deployment is no longer a supported current target.

Historical Deployment

The demo ran on an EC2 instance behind Caddy, which handled TLS automatically, with the public URL provided by sslip.io so no real domain was needed. The app container had no public port of its own; Caddy was the only thing facing the internet.

The build strategy was not the first plan. Building the image directly on the EC2 box failed: the instance ran out of disk space partway through, because the image bakes in PyTorch and the embedding model, and those layers are large. The fix was to move the heavy work off the box. Build the ARM image once on a local machine, push it to Docker Hub, and have EC2 only ever pull the finished image. That solved the disk problem outright and made deployments faster and more repeatable, because the slow model-baking step happens once on capable hardware instead of on every deploy.

Current Limitations

The latest Phase 5 workflow has not completed a qualified live article end to end. Ambiguous anchors and unresolved material claims can stop a run. Recovery and proposed PR #11 changes still need their own live qualification, and the old deployment is not a current target.

What I Learned

Building this taught me to treat a green test, a good-looking score and a successful model response as different pieces of evidence. I can make the system stop when its evidence is incomplete and preserve a human decision before publication. The remaining work is to demonstrate those guarantees across complete live runs, not assume that an offline pass proves them.
